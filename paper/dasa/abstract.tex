\begin{abstract}
On result day, lakhs of students try to open an examination board's website at
the same moment, and these websites often fail. Boards need evidence to decide how to build them.
We built
ResultBench, a testbed that runs eleven designs of a result website on the
same two processor threads, replays a result-day surge of students who wait and
press refresh, and checks every answer. On one
machine, a website that builds every page from a database served every
student within five seconds at the load it was sized for, but only 30.8
percent at twice that load; splitting it into mirror sites or adding an empty
cache did not help. Preparing every result in advance served every student
at ten times that load and used 12.5 to
57.8 times less processor time per student, after less than ten seconds of
preparation. No design returned a wrong result, and old results after a
correction lasted more than a fraction of a second only where cached copies
were not updated. Because each
student asks for a different result, measures that protect a cache from many
identical requests changed nothing, while filling the cache before release
served everyone. Impatient students who kept refreshing kept an unprotected
website overloaded. A virtual waiting room in front of that website served
98.3 percent of students instead of 43.3 percent, but only after waits of
minutes. We turn these measurements into a decision
guide for examination boards and release the testbed as open source.
\end{abstract}
