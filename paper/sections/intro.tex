\section{Introduction}

When an Indian examination board declares results, lakhs of students try to
open the result website at the same time~\cite{ht2021ssc}. The release time is
announced in advance, almost every request asks for one roll number, and the
results do not change while students read them. Few web workloads are simpler.
Yet result websites keep failing on result day. The chairman
of JEE Advanced 2016 described the portal receiving about \num{10000} requests
per second against a server that could hold \num{2000}~\cite{pagalguy2016jee};
on Maharashtra SSC result day in 2021 schools reported that at least half of
their students could not see results until evening~\cite{ht2021ssc}, and the
state's education minister apologised~\cite{careerindia2021ssc}. The problem is
not Indian alone: Kenya's examination council reported up to \num{300000} hits
per second on its results portal~\cite{knec2025x,k24knec2025}, and in 2025 WAEC
in Nigeria withdrew its result
checker over \emph{wrong} results rather than slow ones~\cite{icir2025waec}.

The tools that could prevent these failures are well known: caching or
pre-generating dynamic pages~\cite{challenger1999scalable,labrinidis2000webview},
protecting caches against stampedes~\cite{vattani2015xfetch,nishtala2013memcache},
admission control under overload~\cite{welsh2001seda,elnikety2004gatekeeper,chen2005news},
and avoiding retries that keep an overloaded system
down~\cite{bronson2021metastable,huang2022metastable}. Companies also sell
``virtual waiting rooms'' that hold excess visitors in a queue during traffic
surges~\cite{cloudflare2026wr,aws2022vwr}. What is missing is a measured,
like-for-like comparison of these options for result portals. The closest
recent studies compare a database-only design with Redis cache-aside for
university academic systems under a fixed number of concurrent users, one with
a real PostgreSQL database and Redis~\cite{putranto2026redis} and one as a
prototype in which database
and cache latencies are simulated by fixed delays~\cite{tofan2026cacheaside}.
Neither evaluates pre-computed serving or waiting rooms, models an open-loop
surge of arriving students who refresh, or checks the answers returned under
load. WebQ evaluated a virtual queue in front of an overloaded web
server~\cite{doshi2015webq}, but, to our knowledge, no study has compared
waiting rooms with other designs under an announced surge of students who
refresh.

This paper asks four questions about portals that publish read-only results at
a pre-announced time:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (architecture):} Under the same hardware budget, how far does each common design scale, and at what resource cost?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (waiting rooms):} Does a waiting room improve students' outcomes, and is it fair when students refresh?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (correctness):} Do designs return wrong, missing or stale results under load, after corrections, or when release precedes pre-computation?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (caches and refreshes):} Is cache-aside's cold-start weakness a stampede, and how much do student refreshes amplify load?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are:
(1)~\textbf{ResultBench}, an open, reproducible testbed with eleven portal
designs under one resource budget, an open-loop load generator with a
parameterised model of how students refresh, and an oracle that checks every
response against ground truth;
(2)~a controlled comparison of database-backed, multi-mirror, cache-aside,
pre-computed key-value and pre-computed static designs;
(3)~a controlled comparison of virtual waiting rooms with the other designs
under a result-day surge, including queue order and ticket handling;
(4)~measurements of correctness failures that only appear on result day;
and (5)~a decomposition of cache-aside misses that shows why the usual
stampede defences do little for per-student keys.
