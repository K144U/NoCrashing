# Narrowing the gap keeps the thesis defensible

The gap claimed in Section 2.3 of the thesis plan survives deep research, but only as a narrow conjunction, not as the broad "nobody has compared these architectures" claim it can be read as. No source found by any of the four research strands measures DB-backed, cache-aside, pre-computed, and pre-computed plus waiting-room designs side by side for a result-publication workload, with correctness checked and cost measured. However, three of the gap's parts are already partly covered. **Two 2026 Indonesian papers already compare a plain database design against Redis cache-aside for a university academic portal under high concurrency** ([Putranto & Pinandito, J-PTIIK](https://j-ptiik.ub.ac.id/index.php/j-ptiik/article/view/15978); [Tofan et al., JIP](https://jurnal.polinema.ac.id/index.php/jip/article/view/9675)). A 1999–2004 database and web-systems literature already compared on-demand, cached and pre-materialized page serving ([Labrinidis & Roussopoulos, SIGMOD 2000](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/opsis/papers/index.html)) and front-proxy admission control ([Elnikety et al., WWW 2004](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2004/proceedings/docs/1p276.pdf)). Retry amplification is an established failure mechanism with recent top-venue work ([Huang et al., OSDI 2022](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi22/presentation/huang-lexiang)). Per question, RQ1, RQ2 and RQ4 are **partly answered**, and RQ3 is **answered for admission control in general but open for virtual waiting rooms**. The open-loop load method is established practice and is not a contribution. The plan's evidence base also needs repair before a mentor or examiner sees it. The only "portal coped" example (NIC Odisha) could not be confirmed at all. The "closest recent work" (Baroro) could not be found. The practitioner source is a LinkedIn profile, not a post. Two of the eight technical references cannot be found, and two more are mis-described or cited through slides and blogs. One caveat applies to everything below: almost every cited host was blocked by this session's network, so most verification rests on search-engine titles and snippets, not on opened pages.

## Verdict: the gap holds as a conjunction, not as a blanket claim

Read literally, the Section 2.3 gap is a conjunction of six properties: four specific architectures, one shared harness, a result-day surge, a correctness check, cost measurement, and the result-publication domain. **No academic paper, thesis, vendor case study or open-source project combines them.** The academic search found no peer-reviewed load test or architecture comparison for any national or board result-publication portal, whether CBSE, NIC, a state board, AKTU, Anna University, JAMB, WAEC or KNEC. Existing academic work falls into three groups: single-system JMeter tests of admission or college websites ([Putri et al., IEEE SIET 2017](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8304099/); [Dave & Bhanushali, IJSR 2024](https://www.ijsr.net/getabstract.php?paperid=SR24121010827)), user surveys that report congestion when results are released ([Lubanga, SCECSAL 2018](https://www.scecsal.org/publications/papers2018/040_lubanga_2018.pdf)), and SMS result-checking designs. The industry search also found no published measured comparison. Practitioners publish single-architecture success stories, vendor before-and-after claims for waiting rooms, and incident statements. A GitHub repository search for "exam result portal load test" returned zero repositories ([query](https://github.com/search?q=exam+result+portal+load+test&type=repositories)).

The conjunction is therefore novel. A conjunction is a weak novelty claim, though, because an examiner will test each part separately. Four parts of the plan's wording also overstate the case. First, the plan's own surge is not "replayed". Section 5 builds a synthetic, parameterised ramp-burst-decay shape. No public result-day trace exists to replay, because no NIC, CBSE, NTA or DigiLocker source publishes request rates ([CBSE result-access guide](https://www.cbse.gov.in/cbsenew/documents/HOW%20TO%20ACCESS%20CLASS%20X%20AND%20XII%20RESULT.pdf); [results.nic.in](https://results.nic.in/)). Second, "cost" in Section 6.3 means resource cost (CPU-seconds, memory, database connections, preparation time), not money, and the gap should say so. Third, Section 2.3 says "mostly old" for the flash-crowd literature. That is true of flash-crowd *measurement* studies, but false for the mechanisms the thesis tests, which are active topics at OSDI, NSDI and HotOS through 2025. Fourth, the bullet saying college load-testing papers "test a single design" is no longer true once the two Indonesian Redis papers are counted.

The table hides one analytical point that matters for RQ2. It is an inference, not a sourced finding. Section 5.4 makes uniform roll-number lookups the baseline, and each student looks up mainly their own result. So under a cold cache, nearly every first request is a miss on a *distinct* key, not one of "many simultaneous misses on the same keys" as H2 assumes. In the uniform case, design B's database load on a cold cache roughly equals the rate at which new students arrive. B's advantage comes only from repeat refreshes by the same student, plus hot keys in the skewed variant. As a rough check, the unverified NIC Odisha figures (1,846,050 hits for about 4 lakh candidates) work out to about 4.6 hits per candidate, which suggests some repeat traffic but not much. This is the researchers' arithmetic on unconfirmed numbers. Stampede mitigations such as coalescing and XFetch should therefore matter mostly in the skewed-key condition, and H2 should be split. In the uniform case, B's cold-start penalty is a miss-ratio problem. In the skewed case, it is a stampede problem. Separating these two is a sharper and more original contribution than showing that pre-computation beats a cold cache. Pre-computation wins that comparison by design, because it has no miss path.

## Closest work: two 2026 Indonesian Redis papers and a 1999–2004 lineage

The closest work in both method and domain was published in Indonesian venues in 2026. The plan does not mention either paper. Putranto and Pinandito tested a SIAKAD class-schedule API (Express.js on PostgreSQL, with a three-table JOIN) against a Redis cache-aside version, using JMeter at 1,000, 5,000 and 10,000 concurrent users. At 10,000 users, **SQL averaged about 27.59 s with a 39.41% error rate, while Redis averaged about 95.77 ms with 0% errors**, and a Mann-Whitney U test gave p < 0.05 ([J-PTIIK](https://j-ptiik.ub.ac.id/index.php/j-ptiik/article/view/15978); [Brawijaya repository](https://repository.ub.ac.id/id/eprint/254925/)). Tofan, Parlika and Krispriyanto built a Flask prototype of a course-registration (KRS) module, motivated by "extreme traffic spikes at the start of semester". They *simulated* database latency (500 ms) and cache latency (5 ms) instead of running real MySQL and Redis, and reported **507.52 ms vs 9.12 ms average latency** under Locust with 1,000 users ([JIP 12(3):493–502](https://jurnal.polinema.ac.id/index.php/jip/article/view/9675)). The researchers rated both papers about 3–4 out of 10 for overlap with the thesis. They share the core experiment (DB-only vs cache-aside, open-source load generator, developing-country academic portal). They differ in workload (schedule and registration data is mutable, while results are immutable), number of arms (2 vs 4), load model (fixed concurrency vs a surge shape), correctness (none seen) and cost (none seen). This assessment comes from abstracts only. Whether either paper checks payload correctness or measures cost is **unconfirmed**, and that determines how the gap should be worded.

The second line of prior work is older and comes from database and systems venues. An examiner with a database background will expect it. WebView materialization compared serving pages as virtual views, as views materialized in the DBMS, and as pages pre-generated at the web server, using both a cost model and an implemented system ([Labrinidis & Roussopoulos, SIGMOD 2000, pp. 367–378, via UMD Opsis](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/opsis/papers/index.html); [academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/6487361/WebView_Materialization)). That maps closely onto designs A, B and C. IBM's Data Update Propagation system cached dynamic pages for the 1998 Winter Olympics site ([Challenger, Iyengar & Dantzig, INFOCOM 1999, pp. 294–303, dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/pid/10/1970.html)). For design D, Gatekeeper is the closest academic analogue: an admission-control proxy that needed no changes to the server stack. On TPC-W it gave **up to 14x better response time and about 10% more throughput under overload** ([Elnikety et al., WWW 2004](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2004/proceedings/docs/1p276.pdf)). In Chen and Heidemann's adaptive flash-crowd protection, detection took about 20 s, and **response packet drops fell from 25% to 2% when about 32% of requests were discarded** ([ISI PDF](https://ant.isi.edu/~johnh/PAPERS/Chen03b.pdf)). Caching during simulated flash crowds gave "orders of magnitude" savings ([Ari et al., MASCOTS 2003](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~elm/Papers/mascots03flash.pdf)).

Practitioner work narrows the novelty in a different way. It shows that every design under test is already deployed, so the thesis has to argue for *measurement*, not for invention. Waiting rooms are commodity products: Cloudflare offers FIFO, random and lottery queueing ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/en-gb/application-services/products/waiting-room/)), AWS publishes an open-source virtual waiting room ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/solutions/latest/aws-virtual-waiting-room/welcome.html)), and Vercel publishes a FIFO design ([GitHub](https://github.com/vercel-labs/nextjs-waiting-room/blob/main/docs/waiting-room-scale-architecture.md)). Queue-it reports university deployments, with **80% fewer support requests and 38% lower scaling cost** at King's College London. These are vendor-reported business metrics, not system measurements ([Queue-it](https://queue-it.com/blog/university-registrations-online-queue/)). A 2024 Radboud bachelor's thesis built a distributed virtual waiting room and ran throughput tests ([Radboud](https://www.cs.ru.nl/bachelors-theses/2024/Josja_Koopmans___1071047___Distributed_Virtual_Waiting_Room.pdf)). Hotstar pre-warmed capacity before scheduled cricket matches because reactive autoscaling was too slow ([AWS re:Invent 2019](https://d1.awsstatic.com/events/reinvent/2019/Scaling_Hotstar.com_for_25_million_concurrent_viewers_CMY302.pdf)). China's 12306 rail ticketing system cut backend queries with dedicated query clusters and data synchronization, which is conceptually design C ([Railway Sciences 2023](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/rs-01-2023-0005/full/html)). Indian boards already use a strategy the plan does not model. Kerala SSLC, for example, publishes results across at least five official portals plus apps, a multi-origin mirror approach ([Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/kerala-sslc-result-2023-live-updates-class-10th-results-announced-keralaresults-nic-in-how-to-check-direct-link-toppers-news/amp)). This mirror strategy is the real-world status quo and deserves at least a discussion paragraph.

The plan's claimed "closest recent work", the Baroro study of Philippine enrollment portals, **could not be found anywhere**. Name searches returned no scholarly record, and the academia.edu profile the plan cites could not be fetched. The only candidate is an ACM paper on evaluating a Philippine institution's enrollment information system, with unknown authors ([ACM DL 10.1145/3698062.3698084](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3698062.3698084)). The other Philippine enrollment literature found consists of usability and ISO-25010 surveys, not load experiments ([NEUST assessment](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/349670477_Students'_Assessment_of_the_Online_Enrollment_System_of_Nueva_Ecija_University_of_Science_and_Technology_An_Experienced_Based)). Even if Baroro's paper turns up, the Indonesian Redis papers are closer, so Section 2.3 should replace it.

## A defensible gap statement and the work it now requires

The suggested replacement for the bolded gap in Section 2.3 is:

> **The gap.** Result publication is a scheduled, read-only, key-addressed surge on immutable data, and the failures it produces are documented only in press reports. Prior academic-portal studies compare at most a database-backed design against a cache-aside design, under fixed synthetic concurrency and for mutable schedule or registration data (Putranto & Pinandito 2026; Tofan et al. 2026). The classic work on materialized web views and admission control (Labrinidis & Roussopoulos 2000; Elnikety et al. 2004) predates modern key-value stores and open-loop load generators. No published study we found evaluates pre-computed key-value or static-file serving and a virtual waiting room alongside these designs, in one reproducible harness, under an open-loop result-day surge model with user retries, while checking every response against ground truth and measuring resource cost. This thesis fills that gap for the result-lookup workload. It does not claim to discover that caching, pre-computation or admission control help in general.

This wording does four things. It cites the closest work instead of hiding it. It says "surge model" instead of "replayed surge", because no trace exists. It says "resource cost" to match Section 6.3. It moves the novelty claim from "nothing exists" to "this combination, for this workload, has not been measured". Section 1 should be adjusted to match, and so should the bullets in Section 2.3. Remove "mostly old". Change "test a single design" to "test a single design, except two 2026 two-arm cache studies". Replace Baroro.

The research questions should be tightened as follows. RQ1 should report where each design's success rate falls below the deadline targets at each load multiple. "A fails first" is close to true by construction, so the ordering alone is not a finding. RQ2 should be split into the uniform-key case (a miss-ratio problem) and the skewed-key case (a stampede problem), and should ask how much of the gap coalescing, jitter and XFetch close. RQ3 should ask whether a waiting room adds value in front of design C at all, judged by resource cost for the same SLO, wait-time distribution, and FIFO fairness when users refresh. No peer-reviewed work answers this. RQ4 should adopt the trigger-and-sustaining-effect framing of Huang et al. and add a hysteresis test. Section 5.1 should cite Schroeder et al., call the workload "partly open", and report k6 dropped iterations so that coordinated omission is caught. Retry parameters have no empirical source, because no study measures human refresh behaviour during result or ticket surges. They must be stated as assumptions and varied. The World Cup '98 trace (5–10x ramp before scheduled events) and the JEE 2016 5x overload are the best available anchors for calibrating the surge shape. The KNEC, CoWIN (2.7 million hits per minute, [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/80-lakh-register-for-covid-19-vaccination-in-first-three-hours-on-wednesday-980206.html)) and HealthCare.gov (250,000 users seen vs 50–60k expected, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/11/07/healthcare-gov-could-only-handle-1100-users-on-the-eve-of-launch)) figures supply order-of-magnitude context. Section 8 should add two threats to validity: bot and DDoS traffic, illustrated by the 2016 Australian eCensus, which failed from DDoS and network faults while real load stayed "well within capacity" ([ParlInfo](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;query=Id:%22publications/tabledpapers/a41f4f25-a08e-49a7-9b5f-d2c8af94f5c5%22)), and authentication bottlenecks, illustrated by Quebec 2026. It should also note that the multi-mirror strategy Indian boards actually use is not among the four designs.

The Section 13 search log should add these queries: "WebView materialization", "dynamic content caching" with "Olympics", "virtual waiting room" or "online queue" with "evaluation", "metastable failure", "SIAKAD" with "Redis" or "cache-aside", `"result portal" load`, and `"examination result" website JMeter`. ## Conclusion

The thesis is publishable-grade in its narrow form, but its novelty sits in different places than the plan suggests. Comparing a database against a cache is not the contribution, because two 2026 papers did that. Showing that pre-computation beats caching is not the contribution either; that was argued in 2000 and holds by design. The defensible contributions are three. The first is the first measured evaluation of a virtual waiting room in front of an already-cheap read path, including its fairness under refresh. The second is a correctness oracle on a workload where a wrong answer has already forced a national exam body (WAEC) to pull its portal. The third is the observation that, for uniform roll-number lookups, cache-aside's cold-start weakness is a miss-ratio problem, not a stampede problem. Together with the absence of any public result-portal benchmark harness, these give the thesis a cleaner story than "nobody has compared these designs".

The biggest risk is not a hidden competing paper. It is the evidence base. The plan's only success story (NIC Odisha), its stated closest work (Baroro) and its retry claim (the LinkedIn post) are all unverified, and each one is cited for a specific purpose. If they are fixed in the first two weeks of November, as Section 10 of the plan already schedules, the gap statement above will hold up in a viva. If they are left as they are, an examiner can dismiss the motivation in a single question.
